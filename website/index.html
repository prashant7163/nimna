  • Connection times
  • Ciphertext sizes and timing
  • An opaque routing ID per channel

What it never sees

  • Names, numbers, or accounts — none exist
  • Who you are to each other
  • Any message history — there is none to see

Live-only, by design

Not a worse messenger. Not a messenger at all.

Nimna is a text call. Both of you are on the line, or nothing moves. There is no inbox filling up on a server, because there is no inbox — and no server that keeps one.

Write while they're away and it simply waits on your phone, and goes through the moment you're both on. To everyone else, nothing happened at all.

Discretion, as facts

Quiet on the outside. Yours on the inside.

App lock

PIN or biometric on open. Locked, the app shows nothing — not even how many lines you have.

Disappearing messages

You set a timer per message; both apps delete on schedule. A screenshot is still a screenshot — we say that plainly, in the app, where you set the timer.

Panic wipe

One held press destroys keys and messages on your phone, irrecoverably. There is no backup anywhere to recover from. That is the point.

Content-free notifications

Off by default. If you opt in, a nudge says "New activity" — no name, no line, no content, ever.

Photos that don't whisper

Location, device, and time are stripped from photos on your phone, before encryption. The photographed contract shouldn't say where it was photographed.

Files, phone to phone

Photos and documents up to 5 MB travel the live line like everything else. What you receive stays inside nimna — never your gallery, never a cloud roll. Taking it out is a deliberate act.

What nimna does not protect you from.

Honesty is the feature. Here is the whole threat model, in plain words — including the parts that aren't flattering.

Someone watching the network

They can see that your phone talked to the nimna relay, when, and roughly how much. They cannot see words. If "talking to nimna at all" is dangerous for you, nimna is not enough.

Traffic analysis

The relay necessarily sees IPs and timing. A powerful observer could correlate who talks when. We say this because it's true; future versions aim to narrow it.

The other person

Nimna protects the line, not the human at the far end. Nimna can't stop a screenshot — it will always tell the other person one was taken, and that's the whole protection: notice, not prevention. It only notices inside the app; a second camera pointed at a screen is invisible to any software. And anything you send can simply be retold. No app can fix trust.

A seized, unlocked phone

Whatever is on the screen is on the screen. The app lock, timers, and wipe narrow the window — they do not eliminate it.

Pairing over a hostile channel

If someone intercepts your code before your friend uses it, they could pair in their place. That's why codes die in 24 hours, work once, and why both apps show a safety phrase to read aloud after pairing.

The push trade, if you opt in

Push is off by default, per line. Turn it on and a knock can wake your phone through Apple's or Google's push service — carrying only "New activity." They learn a contentless delivery happened to your device; the relay momentarily links your line to a wake-up address, uses it once, and forgets it. Keeps no list. That transient linkage is the full price of push — leave it off and no third party ever touches the line.

What we can't hand over

If we are ever compelled to produce your messages, we cannot — not as a policy, but as a property of the design. There is nothing stored to produce. The compelled-relay scenario yields ciphertext routing metadata at most, and only for connections live at that moment.

Questions, answered plainly

FAQ

Why must we both be online?

Because the alternative is a server that holds your messages while you're away — and a server that holds messages is a server that can leak, be breached, or be compelled. Nimna is a text call: live when you're both on the line, silent otherwise. The inconvenience is the security.

What happens if I write while they're offline?

Your message waits on your phone — only there — marked "waiting." When you're next both on the line, it goes through by itself. Optionally, they get a contentless nudge that says "New activity" and nothing more.

Can you read my messages?

Architecturally, no. Messages are encrypted on your phone and decrypted on theirs; the relay routes opaque ciphertext and keeps nothing. You don't have to take our word for it — read the relay's source.

What do you know about me?

We don't know who you are. There is no account, so there is nothing to know. The relay transiently sees what any internet service's pipe sees — IP addresses, timing, data sizes — in memory, unlogged, while you're connected. The full list is on the security page, including the unflattering parts.

Why is there no signup?

A line is defined by two keys that live on your two phones — nothing else identifies it. An account would be a list of users, and a list of users is a thing that can be demanded, sold, or stolen. So there isn't one.

What if I lose my phone?

Your lines die with it — cryptographically, permanently, by design. A line is its two keys; the keys lived only on that phone, encrypted behind its lock and excluded from cloud backups. Whoever finds the phone gets nothing, and so do we — any way to resurrect your lines would be a stored identity that outlives the keys, which is exactly the thing nimna refuses to keep. This is the zero-storage promise keeping itself honest.

Getting back costs one message: you already know each other outside nimna — that's how the code traveled the first time — so you pair again with a new code. Your subscription is the one thing that survives, restored through your app-store account on the new phone.

Do disappearing messages really disappear?

Both apps delete them on the schedule the sender set — photos and documents included, same timer, same rule. But we won't pretend a timer beats physics: a screenshot is still a screenshot. What nimna adds is notice — take a screenshot and the other person is always told. The app says all of this where you set the timer. Treat it as a courtesy between two people who trust each other, not a guarantee against one who doesn't.

Is this legal?

Yes. End-to-end encryption is ordinary technology — it protects your banking, your video calls, and the messaging apps on billions of phones. Nimna applies the same mathematics with less storage, not more secrecy. Follow your local laws; nimna is a tool for privacy, which is a normal thing to want.

What does it cost?

Joining is free forever, and a joined line talks both ways. Creating lines takes a yearly subscription — one subscription, as many lines as you like, priced by your app store in your currency (the store is the only place the number appears). And every line you create is permanent: if the subscription lapses, everything you've made keeps working — a lapse only pauses creating new ones.

Can I send photos and files?

Yes — photos and documents up to 5 MB, first-class, over the same live line. Photos are compressed by default (original quality is a toggle) and scrubbed of location, device, and time on your phone, before encryption. What you receive lives inside nimna's encrypted store — never your gallery, never a cloud photo roll — until you deliberately export it. The sender's disappearing timer applies to media exactly as to text.

What do Apple and Google see?

If you leave push off: nothing, ever — the line touches no third party. If you opt in on a line, their push services deliver the two words "New activity" to your phone when someone knocks — no sender, no line name, no content. They learn a contentless delivery happened, nothing more. The relay uses your wake-up address once per knock and immediately forgets it; there is no token list anywhere. That trade is why push is off by default.

Why can't I try creating a line before paying?

Deliberately. Receiving is the free trial — join a line someone made and you get the entire product, forever, for nothing. Keeping creation paid-only also means every line in existence traces back to a real, store-verified payment — one of the structural reasons nimna is a poor tool for strangers and spam: no discovery, strictly two people, and a paid creator behind every line.

Can I run my own relay?

The relay is open source under AGPL-3.0 and the protocol spec is published alongside it, so nothing stops you technically — and the AGPL means anyone operating a modified relay as a service must publish their changes. v1 of the app speaks to the hosted relay; bring-your-own-relay is planned. One honest detail: push credentials are deployment configuration, never part of the code — a self-hosted relay brings its own Apple/Google credentials or runs push-less.
